If you've called your builder about a stucco crack, a sticking door, or drywall separation and gotten a "that's outside our warranty" response, you're running into one of the most common — and least understood — gaps in production home ownership.

## How Builder Warranties Actually Work

Most production builders (DR Horton, Lennar, KB Home, Pulte, and others) offer a tiered warranty: a short workmanship warranty covering the first year or two, a longer warranty on major systems, and a structural warranty that typically runs somewhere between **1 and 10 years**, depending on the builder and your state. Once that structural window closes, the builder has no further contractual obligation — even for issues, like foundation settling, that are a direct result of how the home was built.

## What Usually Falls Into the Gap

- **Stress cracks and drywall separation** as the home settles into its lot after the first several years
- **Stucco cracking or delamination** tied to that same settling movement
- **Foundation and slab issues** that show up gradually rather than immediately after closing
- **Layout limitations** — a builder-grade kitchen or a floor plan that doesn't fit your family isn't a warranty issue at all; it's a renovation decision

None of this means the builder did something wrong. It means production construction is optimized for consistent, fast delivery across an entire subdivision, and the warranty structure reflects that — it was never designed to cover a home's full lifespan.

## Why This Creates an Opportunity, Not Just a Problem

Homeowners in this position often assume their only options are an expensive insurance claim (which usually doesn't apply — most policies exclude normal settling) or living with the issue. In practice, most post-warranty repair and renovation work is straightforward for a contractor who's actually worked in production-built subdivisions before, because the construction methods and floor plans repeat across thousands of homes.

## What to Do When You're Past the Warranty Window

1. **Get an honest diagnosis first.** Not every crack is structural, and not every structural issue is expensive to fix — but you need someone who can tell the difference before you spend money.
2. **Ask whether the contractor has worked in production subdivisions specifically.** General remodelers without that experience often quote higher and take longer because they're learning your floor plan as they go.
3. **Get a written, itemized estimate** before any work starts, especially for foundation, structural, or stucco repair where the visible crack is rarely the whole story.

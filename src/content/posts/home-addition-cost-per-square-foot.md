If you've outgrown your production-built home, an addition is almost always the more cost-effective path compared to moving or building new — because you're already starting with a foundation, a roofline, and utility connections in place. Here's what that actually costs, broken down by the numbers homeowners ask us most.

## The Short Answer

Most single-story additions on a tract-built home run **$180–$400 per square foot fully finished**. A 400 sq ft (20x20) addition typically lands between **$70,000 and $160,000**. A full 1,000 sq ft addition — enough for a primary suite or a couple of extra bedrooms — usually runs **$150,000–$350,000+**, depending on foundation type, finish level, and whether it's a second story.

## What Actually Drives the Cost

- **Foundation type.** A slab addition tied into your existing footprint is the cheapest option. A raised or pier foundation, or anything requiring significant excavation, adds cost quickly.
- **HVAC and electrical tie-ins.** Extending ductwork and panel capacity into new square footage is consistently one of the most expensive line items on the quote — and one production-home floor plans don't always leave room for.
- **Roofline matching.** Tying a new addition's roof into your existing trusses so it reads as original construction, rather than a bolted-on box, takes more labor than a flat lean-to roof — but it's the difference between an addition that adds resale value and one that looks like an afterthought.
- **Second story vs. single story.** Adding a second story requires confirming your existing foundation and framing can bear the additional load, which sometimes means structural reinforcement before the addition itself begins.

## Why Production-Home Experience Matters Here

Tract homes are built to a repeated plan, which is actually an advantage once you know it: we've typically already worked inside the same or a very similar floor plan, so we know where the load-bearing walls, plumbing stacks, and truss layout are before we open anything up. That translates to fewer surprise change orders once demo starts — which is where a lot of addition budgets go sideways with contractors who haven't worked in production subdivisions before.

## What's the Most Expensive Part?

Foundation work and HVAC/electrical tie-ins are consistently the biggest line items, followed by roofing work to match the existing structure. Finish-level choices (flooring, fixtures, trim) matter less to the total than most homeowners expect — the structural and systems work is where the real cost lives.
